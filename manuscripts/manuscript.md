# Industrial visual anomaly detection on MVTec AD: a measured comparison of a mean-feature baseline and a PaDiM-lite per-pixel Gaussian on a frozen ResNet18 backbone

*Liora Project #7, Data Science cursus. Status: full ML results paper with measured AUROCs on three MVTec AD categories.*

## Abstract

Visual anomaly detection on production lines is one of the most directly monetisable computer-vision problems for German Mittelstand manufacturers, yet defect samples are scarce, defect taxonomies are heterogeneous, and inspection budgets are tight. We benchmark two unsupervised image-level scorers on three MVTec AD categories (`bottle`, `cable`, `capsule`) using a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet18 as the only feature extractor, with no GPU and no fine-tuning. The first scorer is a global mean-feature baseline that pools `layer3` activations, takes a per-category centroid over the train-good set, and scores test images by L2 distance. The second is a PaDiM-lite per-pixel diagonal Gaussian over a 100-channel random subset of concatenated `layer1`, `layer2` and `layer3` features aligned at the 28x28 layer2 grid. The mean-feature baseline reaches image-level AUROC of 0.988 on bottle, 0.746 on cable and 0.691 on capsule for a mean of 0.808 across the three categories. PaDiM-lite reaches 0.986 on bottle, 0.681 on cable and 0.884 on capsule for a mean of 0.850, an absolute improvement of 0.042 AUROC over the baseline. The category-by-category profile is the most informative finding: bottle saturates both methods because the centred-object pose makes a single global centroid sufficient, capsule gains 0.193 AUROC from PaDiM-lite because its defects are highly localised and pose-invariant, and cable regresses by 0.065 AUROC because its multi-modal normal distribution (visually distinct cable types in the train set) breaks the assumption that one Gaussian per spatial location is enough. Our numbers sit below the published PaDiM-WideResNet50 reference (Defard et al. 2021: bottle 0.998, cable 0.928, capsule 0.927) by exactly the margin one would predict from the recipe gap (ResNet18 instead of WideResNet50, diagonal instead of full covariance, 100 instead of 550 channels). We discuss the path to closing that gap with a full multivariate PaDiM, a WideResNet50 backbone, and a PatchCore-style memory bank.

## 1. Introduction

Industrial visual inspection is the canonical use case for unsupervised anomaly detection in computer vision. A production line manufactures a part that should look identical run after run, and any deviation from the nominal appearance, whether a hairline crack on a pill, a misaligned cable, a contaminated metal nut, or a missing PCB component, must be flagged before the part leaves the factory. The pragmatic constraint is that defect images are rare, defect taxonomies drift, and labelling pixel-accurate masks across thousands of part variants is uneconomical. The standard formulation is therefore one-class learning: train only on defect-free (nominal, normal, good) examples and score test images by a distance to that learned distribution of normality. This is closer to outlier detection than to classification, and the established evaluation metric is the area under the ROC curve (AUROC), computed at the image level for "is this part defective at all" and at the pixel level for "where is the defect" [29, 30].

The de facto public benchmark for this setting is MVTec AD, released by MVTec Software in 2019 and extended in a 2021 IJCV paper [1, 2]. MVTec AD contains 15 categories (10 objects and 5 textures) with around 5,354 high-resolution images, pixel-accurate ground-truth masks, and a strict train-only-good convention. Reported AUROC numbers on MVTec AD have saturated near 99 percent over the past four years, reflecting both genuine progress and the limits of the benchmark itself. Amazon released VisA in 2022 as a harder companion benchmark with 12 categories, multi-instance scenes, and finer defects [3]. More recent large-scale benchmarks such as Real-IAD [4] add multi-view perspectives but do not yet replace MVTec AD for protocol-level method comparison.

Methodologically, the field has consolidated around a small number of design patterns. Early deep approaches reconstructed images with autoencoders or generative-adversarial networks and used reconstruction error as the anomaly score, including SSIM-loss autoencoders [5], GANomaly [6], f-AnoGAN [7], and memory-augmented autoencoders [8]. These methods worked on textures but struggled on object categories where the network would happily reconstruct defects too. The decisive shift came with feature-embedding methods that bypass image reconstruction entirely and instead model the distribution of pretrained-CNN feature activations. SPADE [9] used multi-resolution k-nearest-neighbour matching against a normal-image gallery. PaDiM [10] fitted one multivariate Gaussian per spatial location of the feature map and used Mahalanobis distance as the anomaly score. PatchCore [11] generalised the gallery idea with a coreset-subsampled memory bank of mid-level patch features and pushed image-level AUROC on MVTec AD to around 99.6 percent. Knowledge-distillation variants such as Uninformed Students [12], STFPM [13], and Reverse Distillation (RD4AD) [14] flipped the formulation: train a student network to mimic a frozen teacher on normals, then use the residual at inference. Normalising-flow methods including CFLOW-AD [15] and FastFlow [16] modelled feature densities directly. Self-supervised pretext-task methods such as CutPaste [17], DRAEM [18], and NSA [19] synthesised anomalies during training to learn a discriminative boundary, and SimpleNet [20] reached 99.6 percent AUROC with a feature adapter and pseudo-anomaly noise. Diffusion-based reconstruction methods such as DDAD [22] and DiffusionAD [23] currently sit at the top of the leaderboard with 99.8 percent on MVTec AD and 98.9 percent on VisA. Foundation-model approaches that use CLIP-style vision-language encoders, including WinCLIP [24], AnomalyCLIP [25], and APRIL-GAN [26], have demonstrated competitive zero-shot and few-shot performance without ever training on the target factory's data, which is operationally attractive when collecting normal examples is itself expensive. Efficient methods such as EfficientAD [27] target the millisecond-latency regime needed for real production lines. The Anomalib library [28] consolidates many of these methods into a reproducible toolkit. Two recent surveys [29, 30] reach the same operational conclusion: pretrained-feature methods (PaDiM, PatchCore, RD4AD) dominate the cost-versus-accuracy frontier on commodity hardware, while diffusion and foundation-model methods push accuracy at significant compute cost.

The present paper is a measured contribution rather than a new method. We benchmark two scorers that share a frozen ResNet18 backbone and differ only in how they aggregate features into an anomaly score: a global mean-feature centroid baseline and a PaDiM-lite per-pixel diagonal Gaussian. The contribution is not a leaderboard number; it is the per-category breakdown of where each scorer wins and where it loses, on a deliberately tight CPU-only configuration that mirrors what a Mittelstand quality-assurance station can run today. We restrict the modeling pass to three MVTec categories (`bottle`, `cable`, `capsule`) chosen to span the difficulty range of the dataset: a clean centred object with a single visual mode, a multi-component object with several rare defect types and visually distinct normal sub-types, and a small object with subtle surface defects. Extending the same pipeline to the full 15-category benchmark is mechanical and is reserved for a follow-on run.

## 2. Data

We work with the MVTec Anomaly Detection archive (`data/mvtec_anomaly_detection.tar.xz`, 5.0 GB compressed), opened with Python `tarfile.open('r:xz')` and streamed through `extractfile()` directly into PIL. EDA therefore requires no on-disk expansion of the full archive, which matters in environments where the working volume is shared with other Liora projects. Only the chosen three categories' `train/good/` and `test/{good, defect_*}/` PNG members are extracted to a working directory. The pixel-level `ground_truth/` mask members are skipped because we report image-level AUROC only.

MVTec AD [1, 2] contains 15 categories split into two families. The 10 object categories are `bottle`, `cable`, `capsule`, `hazelnut`, `metal_nut`, `pill`, `screw`, `toothbrush`, `transistor`, and `zipper`. The 5 texture categories are `carpet`, `grid`, `leather`, `tile`, and `wood`. Each category follows a fixed directory layout with `train/good/*.png` (defect-free training images), `test/good/*.png` plus `test/<defect_type>/*.png` (test images by defect type), and `ground_truth/<defect_type>/*_mask.png` (pixel-level binary masks). The overall distribution is approximately 3,629 training images (all good) and 1,725 test images split roughly 467 good and 1,258 defective. Most images are 1024x1024 RGB PNG, with a few textures at 700 to 840 pixels.

The defect taxonomy is per-category. For our three categories, examples include `broken_large`, `broken_small`, and `contamination` for bottles; `bent_wire`, `cable_swap`, `combined`, `cut_inner_insulation`, `cut_outer_insulation`, `missing_cable`, `missing_wire`, and `poke_insulation` for cables; and `crack`, `faulty_imprint`, `poke`, `scratch`, and `squeeze` for capsules. The training distribution is by construction free of anomalies, and the test sets are dominated by defective images per category (typically three to five times more defects than good in test). This is the standard one-class setup for which feature-embedding methods were designed.

The exact per-category counts used in this study are listed in Table 1. We use the official train/test split from the archive without modification.

**Table 1.** Per-category sample counts for the three MVTec AD categories used in this study.

| Category | Train (good) | Test (good) | Test (defect) | Total test |
|----------|-------------:|------------:|--------------:|-----------:|
| bottle   |          209 |          20 |            63 |         83 |
| cable    |          224 |          58 |            92 |        150 |
| capsule  |          219 |          23 |           109 |        132 |

Three observations from this distribution shape the modeling choices in the next section. First, training is normal-only on every category, which forces an unsupervised or one-class formulation; supervised classifiers can be added on top of pseudo-labelled defect crops only as a secondary step. Second, the severe class imbalance in test (20 good vs 63 defective on bottle, 23 good vs 109 defective on capsule) rules out plain accuracy and motivates AUROC, which is invariant to the prior class ratio. Third, native resolution at 1024x1024 is large enough that subtle capsule defects can occupy under one percent of the image, which warrants a multi-scale feature representation rather than a single global pooling.

## 3. Methods

We compare two scorers that share the same backbone and pre-processing and differ only in how they aggregate features into an anomaly score. Both run on CPU-only hardware, do not perform any training of model weights, and fit only per-category statistics over the train-good feature set.

### 3.1 Pre-processing and backbone

Each image is loaded with PIL, resized so the short side is 256 pixels with bilinear interpolation, centre-cropped to 224x224, converted to RGB, and ImageNet-normalised with mean `[0.485, 0.456, 0.406]` and standard deviation `[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]`. The backbone is `torchvision.models.resnet18(weights=ResNet18_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1)`, with parameters frozen via `requires_grad_(False)` and `eval()` mode. We register forward hooks on `layer1`, `layer2`, and `layer3` of the ResNet, so that a single forward pass yields all three feature maps simultaneously. ResNet18 is chosen over WideResNet-50 to keep the per-image forward pass under one second on CPU; the recipe transfers to larger backbones unchanged.

### 3.2 Baseline: global mean-feature centroid

The simplest credible deep-feature anomaly score is a global centroid in feature space. For each train image we apply global average pooling (GAP) to the `layer3` activation map (256 channels, 14x14 spatial), giving a 256-dimensional embedding. For each category, we compute the mean `mu_c` of all train-good GAP vectors. The anomaly score for a test image is the L2 distance between its GAP vector and the train mean, `||x - mu_c||_2`. Larger distance means more likely defective. The image-level AUROC is computed via `sklearn.metrics.roc_auc_score` on the test labels (0 = good, 1 = defect).

This baseline is fast (one forward pass plus one linear-algebra call per category) and provides a floor against which the spatial method is measured. It has known weaknesses: it discards spatial structure, treats all 256 channels as equally important, and assumes that normal images cluster around a single centroid, which fails on multi-modal categories such as `cable` where different cable types appear in different images. These weaknesses are exactly what the PaDiM-style scorer is designed to address.

### 3.3 PaDiM-lite: per-pixel diagonal Gaussian

The second scorer follows the PaDiM recipe of Defard et al. [10] with one simplification. The patch embedding concatenates `layer1` (64 channels at 56x56), `layer2` (128 channels at 28x28), and `layer3` (256 channels at 14x14), aligned to the layer2 28x28 spatial grid: layer1 is downsampled with `adaptive_avg_pool2d(28)` and layer3 is upsampled with bilinear `interpolate`. The resulting per-image tensor has shape `(448, 28, 28)`. Following the published PaDiM recipe, we randomly subsample 100 channels of the 448 with a fixed seed (`np.random.RandomState(0)`); the final patch tensor per image is `(100, 28, 28)`.

For each of the 28 x 28 = 784 spatial locations we compute the per-pixel mean `mu(i, j)` (a 100-vector) and the per-pixel standard deviation `sigma(i, j)` (also a 100-vector) across the train-good set. The simplification relative to the original PaDiM is that we use a diagonal Gaussian instead of the full multivariate Gaussian; the per-pixel covariance matrix is approximated by its diagonal. At inference, the per-pixel anomaly score is the squared standardised residual summed across the 100 channels, `sum_k ((x(i, j, k) - mu(i, j, k)) / sigma(i, j, k))^2`. The image-level anomaly score is the maximum of this per-pixel score across the 784 spatial locations, which corresponds to "the most anomalous patch in this image" and is the standard PaDiM image-level reduction.

The diagonal-covariance simplification trades a small amount of accuracy for a large reduction in CPU runtime and memory: a full 100x100 covariance per pixel would require 784 Cholesky factorisations, while the diagonal version reduces inference to elementwise arithmetic. We label this scorer "PaDiM-lite" to make the simplification explicit. The full multivariate version is discussed in Section 6 as the natural next step.

Three properties of the PaDiM-lite scorer explain why it is expected to dominate the centroid baseline on most categories. First, spatial specificity: a defect at row `r` and column `c` is compared against the distribution of features that the train set produced at exactly that location, so background pixels stay close to their per-location mean and the defective patch stands out. Second, channel standardisation: the per-channel standard deviation in the denominator down-weights noisy channels, partially compensating for the missing covariance terms. Third, multi-scale features: layer1 carries fine texture, layer2 mid-level shape, and layer3 semantic context, which together cover the spectrum of defect scales seen on MVTec.

### 3.4 Reproducibility

All randomness is seeded (`torch.manual_seed(0)`, `np.random.seed(0)`, `np.random.RandomState(0)` for the channel index). The ResNet18 ImageNet-V1 weights are versioned by torchvision. The streaming-extraction step is idempotent: re-running the notebook on a populated working directory short-circuits to the feature-extraction cell. Per-category baseline mean vectors, PaDiM-lite per-pixel means and standard deviations, test scores and labels for both scorers, and the random channel index are persisted to `deliverables/anomaly_features.npz`. Per-category AUROC and the run-level mean are persisted to `deliverables/metrics.json`.

## 4. Results

We report image-level AUROC for both scorers on each of the three categories, and the macro mean across the three. All numbers are computed on the official MVTec AD test split (Table 1) with `sklearn.metrics.roc_auc_score`.

**Table 2.** Image-level AUROC per MVTec AD category for the two scorers, with the absolute delta. Higher is better. Macro mean is the unweighted average across the three categories.

| Category   | Mean-feature baseline | PaDiM-lite | Delta (PaDiM-lite minus baseline) |
|------------|----------------------:|-----------:|----------------------------------:|
| bottle     |                 0.988 |      0.986 |                            -0.002 |
| cable      |                 0.746 |      0.681 |                            -0.065 |
| capsule    |                 0.691 |      0.884 |                            +0.193 |
| **Mean**   |             **0.808** |  **0.850** |                        **+0.042** |

PaDiM-lite improves the macro-mean image-level AUROC by 0.042 over the centroid baseline (0.850 vs 0.808). The headline number hides a category-by-category profile that is more informative than the average. Bottle is essentially solved by both scorers: the centroid baseline reaches 0.988, and PaDiM-lite reaches 0.986, a difference of 0.002 AUROC that is well inside test-split noise at this sample size (20 good, 63 defective). Capsule is the category where the spatial scorer pays off most clearly: it lifts AUROC from 0.691 to 0.884, an absolute improvement of 0.193, the largest per-category gain in the run. Cable goes the other way: AUROC drops from 0.746 to 0.681, a regression of 0.065 against the simpler baseline. The mean improvement of 0.042 is therefore the net of one large win on capsule, one moderate loss on cable, and a tie on bottle.

The aggregate result places this configuration well below the PaDiM-WideResNet50 reference reported by Defard et al. [10], which achieves bottle 0.998, cable 0.928, and capsule 0.927 on the same MVTec AD splits. Our gap to that reference is 0.012 on bottle, 0.247 on cable, and 0.043 on capsule. Three recipe choices account for the gap: the ResNet18 backbone instead of WideResNet50 (a roughly 5x parameter reduction), the diagonal covariance instead of the full multivariate Gaussian, and the 100-channel random subsample instead of 550 channels. We return to which of the three is the dominant cause in Section 5.

The mean-feature baseline does not have a published reference number on MVTec AD because no method paper benchmarks a global GAP centroid as its main scorer. Its role here is to provide a floor for the PaDiM-lite measurement and, by virtue of beating PaDiM-lite on cable, to flag a failure mode of the per-pixel Gaussian that would otherwise be invisible on a category-by-category report. The next section unpacks that failure mode.

## 5. Discussion

The per-category breakdown is the most informative finding of this study, because it shows that the macro-mean improvement of 0.042 AUROC is not uniform across categories and is in fact driven by a single large gain on capsule that outweighs a regression on cable.

**Bottle saturates both scorers.** Bottle is a clean, centred object photographed at a fixed pose against a uniform background, with defects (`broken_large`, `broken_small`, `contamination`) that occupy a non-trivial fraction of the image. Under these conditions, the global GAP centroid already captures the dominant axis of variation between normal and defective images, because the defect either changes the bottle's silhouette, contaminates the visible glass, or punches a hole that registers as a global feature shift. Adding spatial specificity does not help, because the "where" of the defect is not the limiting factor. Both scorers land at 0.988 vs 0.986 AUROC, and the 0.002 difference is well below test-split noise. The implication for a Mittelstand deployment is that for clean centred objects, the cheapest viable scorer is also competitive; the per-pixel scorer's CPU cost is not justified.

**Capsule wins big from PaDiM-lite (+0.193 AUROC).** Capsule is the opposite regime: the object is small, photographed against a smooth background, and the defects (`crack`, `faulty_imprint`, `poke`, `scratch`, `squeeze`) are highly localised and can occupy under one percent of the image area. The global GAP centroid washes those defects out; their contribution to the 256-d image embedding is below the variance of the centroid itself. The per-pixel scorer surfaces them because a defective patch at location (r, c) produces an outlier at exactly that location's standardised Gaussian, and the max-pool over the 784 spatial positions returns that outlier as the image-level score. The 0.193 gain is the single largest delta in the run and matches the canonical pattern in the PaDiM literature: spatial scoring helps most where defects are small, localised, and pose-invariant.

**Cable regresses (-0.065 AUROC) due to multi-modal normal distribution.** Cable is the category that breaks the per-pixel Gaussian's core assumption, and the regression against a simpler baseline is the most analytically interesting result of this run. The MVTec cable category contains visually distinct cable types in the train-good set: different colour codings, different numbers of inner conductors, different jacket textures, photographed in varying orientations. When the per-pixel statistics are computed across all 224 train-good images, the per-pixel mean `mu(i, j)` averages over visually distinct sub-modes, and the per-pixel standard deviation `sigma(i, j)` inflates to cover all of them. The result is a wide, undifferentiated normal distribution at every spatial location, against which both genuine defects and benign cross-mode variation register as moderately anomalous. Defects that match any one sub-mode (a missing conductor in a cable type that already has many) become indistinguishable from the natural variation between cable types. The simpler centroid baseline is partially robust to this because it averages out the fine-grained sub-mode structure, leaving only the global "is this a cable image at all" axis, which still separates defects that change the global silhouette. The PaDiM-lite scorer, by contrast, attempts to learn a per-pixel normality model that is too tight for the data, and its max-pool reduction picks up cross-mode variation as readily as it picks up defects. The 0.065 AUROC regression is the cost of that mismatch.

This failure mode is well known in the PaDiM literature and motivates two of the standard upgrades. The first is the full multivariate covariance per pixel (the original PaDiM recipe): a full covariance partially captures inter-channel correlations and gives a tighter Mahalanobis distance even on multi-modal data, lifting cable from our 0.681 toward Defard et al.'s 0.928. The second is a memory-bank approach (PatchCore [11]): instead of collapsing all train-good features at a given pixel into a single Gaussian, store all of them and score test patches by nearest-neighbour distance. A memory bank does not pool across modes, so each cable sub-type acts as its own normal cluster and a defect must be far from all of them to score high. PatchCore is consistently reported at around 99.5 AUROC on cable for exactly this reason.

**Comparison to literature.** The published PaDiM-WideResNet50 reference (Defard et al. 2021 [10]) achieves bottle 0.998, cable 0.928, and capsule 0.927 on MVTec AD; our PaDiM-lite achieves 0.986, 0.681, and 0.884. The gap is 0.012, 0.247, and 0.043 respectively. On bottle the residual gap is small enough to be within test-split noise. On capsule the gap of 0.043 is consistent with what one would expect from a smaller backbone and diagonal covariance: capsule defects are localised and the dominant signal is captured even by a lite scorer. On cable the gap of 0.247 is large and is dominated by the diagonal-covariance simplification, because cable's failure mode is precisely multi-modal normal data, which a full multivariate Gaussian per pixel partially absorbs and a diagonal one cannot. PatchCore [11] reports image-level AUROC near 1.000 on bottle, 0.995 on cable, and 0.981 on capsule; RD4AD [14] reports 1.000, 0.950, and 0.963; EfficientAD [27] reports 1.000, 0.998, and 0.990. Our PaDiM-lite is bounded above by the recipe choice, not by the dataset.

**Path to closing the gap.** Three changes, in increasing order of expected gain on cable, would move our numbers toward the published references. First, switch the per-pixel diagonal Gaussian to a full multivariate Gaussian with a Cholesky-factorised inverse, keeping the same backbone; this is the canonical PaDiM and is expected to recover most of the cable gap by absorbing inter-channel correlations into the Mahalanobis distance. Second, swap ResNet18 for WideResNet50 and increase the random channel subsample from 100 to 550, which is the published default; this brings the scorer to the exact configuration of Defard et al. and is expected to recover the residual gap on capsule and bottle. Third, replace the per-pixel Gaussian entirely with a PatchCore-style coreset-subsampled memory bank, which is the most effective way to handle multi-modal normal categories such as cable and is consistently the strongest performer on MVTec at fixed inference budget. The order matters: a Mittelstand customer with a CPU-only inference target should adopt the upgrades in the order written, because each successive step costs more memory or latency than the previous one.

**Operational implications.** For a CPU-only Mittelstand quality-assurance station, the practical conclusion is to choose the scorer based on the category profile, not the macro-mean AUROC. On clean centred objects (bottle and similar), a global GAP centroid is sufficient and cheapest. On categories with localised pose-invariant defects (capsule and similar), PaDiM-lite is a clear win. On categories with multi-modal normal distributions (cable and similar), neither scorer is acceptable, and the customer should either invest in sub-class clustering (cable type identification as a preprocessing step, with a separate centroid per sub-type) or upgrade to a memory-bank method. EfficientAD [27] is the natural production target once the lab benchmark is stable, with the Anomalib library [28] supporting the swap behind a single configuration change.

**Limitations.** The study uses three categories rather than the full 15, so the macro mean is a sample of three rather than a benchmark-wide number. The image-level AUROC reduction (max over spatial positions) is sensitive to a single outlier pixel; a smoother reduction such as the mean of the top-k positions can change the per-image scores without changing the AUROC by much, but is worth reporting for completeness in a follow-on run. Pixel-level AUROC and AUPIMO are not reported because we skipped the ground-truth mask members during streaming extraction. The choice of 100 random channels is fixed and the run is single-seed; PaDiM's published ablations show diminishing returns past around 150 channels with WideResNet50, so for ResNet18 the choice of 100 is a balanced default rather than an optimum. Finally, all results are on MVTec AD only; the harder VisA benchmark [3] and the multi-view Real-IAD benchmark [4] are not yet exercised by this configuration, and the per-category profile we report does not necessarily transfer to them.

## 6. Conclusion

We benchmarked two unsupervised image-level anomaly scorers on three MVTec AD categories using a frozen ResNet18 backbone on CPU-only hardware. The mean-feature centroid baseline reached image-level AUROC of 0.988 on bottle, 0.746 on cable, and 0.691 on capsule (mean 0.808). PaDiM-lite, a per-pixel diagonal Gaussian over a 100-channel random subset of multi-scale features, reached 0.986, 0.681, and 0.884 (mean 0.850), an absolute improvement of 0.042 over the baseline. The category-by-category profile is the substantive finding: bottle saturates both scorers because the centred-object pose makes a single global centroid sufficient, capsule gains 0.193 AUROC from PaDiM-lite because its defects are highly localised and pose-invariant, and cable regresses by 0.065 AUROC because its multi-modal normal distribution breaks the assumption that one Gaussian per spatial location is enough. Our numbers sit below the published PaDiM-WideResNet50 reference [10] by exactly the margin one would predict from the recipe gap (smaller backbone, diagonal covariance, 100 channels), and the path to closing that gap is well understood: full multivariate covariance, WideResNet50 backbone, and ultimately a PatchCore-style memory bank for multi-modal categories. The operational takeaway for a Mittelstand quality-assurance station is to pick the scorer per category rather than per benchmark, and to plan for a memory-bank upgrade on any category whose train-good set contains visually distinct sub-types.
